Steiner tutorial · connect 3 seeds

A fixed board that teaches every mechanic. Solve it, then take on the dailies.

  1. Goal. Link all 3 seeds (●) with moss. Drag across squares to grow; drag over moss to erase. Costs: moss 1, spore + free, thorn ! 3, portal ends 1 each, seeds free. Press Check — you pass when every seed is joined into one network.
  2. Rock. Black squares block growth. A rock column splits this board in two, so route around it — over the top or under the bottom.
  3. Thorns. The straight road along the top runs through a thorn (!): 3 for a single square. Detour one square around it instead.
  4. Spores. The + squares are free. Bend a route through them whenever it's cheap.
  5. Portals. The two A squares are one mycelium portal: grow onto both and west and east connect underground for just 2 total, skipping the whole middle. That shortcut is the key move on this board.
  6. Sharing. Never build one road per seed pair. Share trunk routes between all three seeds — the junctions where routes meet are called Steiner points, and sharing is what makes a network short.

Par on this board: — the exact optimum, found by exhaustive search. Match it for a perfect game.

Colouring tutorial · paint 5 nodes

A fixed board that teaches the whole game.

  1. Paint. Pick a colour chip, then tap a node. Tap a node with its own colour to erase. Start by painting all 5 nodes anything valid.
  2. Rule. Neighbours must differ: a red edge means its two ends clash. Everything painted plus no red edges is solved — press Check.
  3. Lower bound. Nodes 1, 2 and 3 are all joined to each other — a triangle. Three mutually adjacent nodes need three different colours, so this board needs at least 3. That trick generalises: a clique of k mutually adjacent nodes always needs k colours, which tells you your target before you start.
  4. Optimality. You start with 3 chips: solve the board and your score equals the clique bound, which proves you can't do better — ★★★. (Use + colour first if you want an easy valid solution, then squeeze down.)
  5. Forcing. A node touching all-but-one colour has only one option left — like unit propagation in SAT solvers. Paint forced nodes first and let the constraints cascade; save guesses for last.
  6. Impossibility. Try it: drop to 2 chips with − colour and press Check. A triangle can't fit in 2 colours (pigeonhole principle) — feeling that impossibility is exactly what ★★★ certifies you escaped.

How scoring works

Pick a colour, then tap a vertex to paint it. Tap again with the same colour to erase. A solution is valid when every vertex is coloured and no edge joins two equal colours. Score = number of distinct colours used. Match the hidden optimum for ★★★. Dailies come from real applications: maps (neighbouring districts differ — the four-colour theorem says no map needs 5), timetables (exams sharing students can't share a slot, laid out in time order), and SAT reductions (a 3-SAT clause compiled into gadgets — colouring the graph solves the formula), plus classic wheels, rings and propagation chains, and Sudoku (every row, column and box is a clique — givens arrive locked, plus a few extra rivalry links).
